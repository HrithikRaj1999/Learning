LECTURE 1 Vectors, row operations, solving \(Ax=b\)

1.1 · Vectors & dot product slides 3–5

A vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a list of \(n\) real numbers. You can do two things with vectors: add them and scale them.
\[\begin{bmatrix}10\\20\\30\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}5\\6\\7\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}15\\26\\37\end{bmatrix}\qquad 3\begin{bmatrix}10\\20\\30\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}30\\60\\90\end{bmatrix}\]
Dot product
\(\langle x,y\rangle=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i\)  ·  \(\langle(1,0,1),(0,1,0)\rangle=0\)
Watch out: a dot product gives a number, not a vector.

1.2 · Elementary row operations slides 6–11

There are three moves that never change the solutions of a system.
MoveNotationRule
Swap\(R_1\leftrightarrow R_3\)exchange two rows
Scale\(R_4\leftarrow 4R_4\)multiply by a non-zero number
Replace\(R_2\leftarrow R_2-4R_1\)add a multiple of another row
\(\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1\\ 4 & -8 & 3\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{R_2\leftarrow R_2-4R_1}\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1\\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}\)
Watch out: the whole row changes, including every sign.

1.3 · REF & RREF slides 12–15

Pivot = the first non-zero entry in a row. Echelon form = pivots form a staircase going right, with zero rows at the bottom.
■****
00■**
000■*
00000
RREF adds two rules
every pivot = 1, and each pivot is the only non-zero entry in its column.
\(\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 2\\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{R_2\leftarrow R_2-R_1}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 2\\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{REF}}\xrightarrow{R_1\leftarrow R_1-R_2}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 & -1\\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{RREF}}\)
Watch out: two rows starting in the same column means it's not echelon yet.

1.4 · Systems: how many solutions? slides 16–25

Write the equations as \(Ax=b\). Work on the augmented matrix \([A\mid b]\). If \(b=0\), the system is homogeneous.
\[\begin{cases}x_1+x_2+x_3=2\\ 2x_1+x_2+x_3=3\end{cases}\iff\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1\\ 2 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}\]
Read it from REF (general rule)
a row \([\,0\;0\;0\mid 5\,]\) → none · a free variable → infinite · pivot in every column → one

1.5 · General solution \(x_p+x_h\) slides 26–30

Every solution = one particular solution + all solutions of \(Ax=0\).
  1. RREF of \([A\mid b]\); pivot columns → basic, the rest → free
  2. \(x_p\): set all free variables = 0, then read off the basic ones
  3. \(x_h\): solve \(Ax=0\), one vector per free variable (that one = 1, the others = 0)
  4. \(x=x_p+\lambda_1h_1+\lambda_2h_2\), then verify
\(\left[\begin{array}{ccccc|c}1 & 3 & 0 & 0 & 3 & 1\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 9 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -4 & 0\end{array}\right]\)   free: \(x_2,x_5\) \[x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\2\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\lambda_1\begin{bmatrix}-3\\1\\0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\lambda_2\begin{bmatrix}-3\\0\\-9\\4\\1\end{bmatrix}\] Check: rank 3 + nullity 2 = 5 columns ✓
Watch out: \(x_p\) solves \(Ax=b\), but each \(h\) solves \(Ax=0\). Don't mix them up.

1.6 · Inverse by Gauss–Jordan slides 31–32

A is invertible exactly when its RREF is \(I\), meaning every column has a pivot.
\[\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}2 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{array}\right]\longrightarrow\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}1 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{array}\right]\]
The left half becomes \(I\) → the right half is \(A^{-1}\).
Watch out: if a zero row appears on the left, A is not invertible.

LECTURE 2 Independence, groups, vector spaces, subspaces, span

2.1 · Linear independence slides 3–7 · Lecture 3a notes

Independent = the only way to reach \(0\) is with all \(\lambda_i=0\). There's no third option: vectors are either independent or dependent.
\[\lambda_1x_1+\lambda_2x_2+\dots+\lambda_kx_k=0\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda_1=\dots=\lambda_k=0\;?\]
Test
Vectors as columns → REF → pivot columns are independent. Each non-pivot column = a combination of the pivot columns to its left.
\(\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 3\\ 2 & 4 & 4\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\boxed{1} & 2 & 3\\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{-2}\end{bmatrix}\): column 2 = 2 × column 1 → dependent
Lecture 3a example
\(x_1=b_1-b_2,\;x_2=2b_1-2b_2\): \(\alpha x_1+\beta x_2=(\alpha+2\beta)b_1+(-\alpha-2\beta)b_2\). Taking \(\alpha=2,\beta=-1\) gives 0 → dependent.
Watch out: any set containing the zero vector is automatically dependent.

2.2 · Abelian group \((G,\otimes)\) slides 8–9

A set with one operation that follows 5 rules.
1Closure
\(x\otimes y\in G\)
2Associative
\((x\otimes y)\otimes z\)
3Identity
\(x\otimes e=x\)
4Inverse
\(x\otimes y=e\)
5Commutative
\(x\otimes y=y\otimes x\)
ExampleGroup?Why
\((\mathbb{Z},+)\)✓ Abelianall 5 rules hold
\((\mathbb{N}_0,+)\)✗no inverse: \(-3\notin\mathbb{N}_0\)
\((\mathbb{Z},\cdot)\)✗2 has no integer inverse

2.3 · Vector space \((V,+,\cdot)\) slides 10–13 · Lecture 2a notes

\((V,+)\) is an Abelian group (the inner operation, addition), plus rules for scalar multiplication (the outer operation).
\(\lambda(x+y)=\lambda x+\lambda y\)  ·  \((\lambda+\psi)x=\lambda x+\psi x\)
\(\lambda(\psi x)=(\lambda\psi)x\)  ·  \(1\cdot x=x\)  ·  zero vector \([0,\dots,0]^{\top}\)
Examples
\(\mathbb{R}^n\) · matrices \(\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) (add and scale entry by entry) · polynomials

2.4 · Subspace test slides 14–17

\(U\subseteq V\) is a subspace if it passes three checks. Every other rule is inherited from V.
STEP 1Non-empty
\(0\in U\)
STEP 2Addition
\(x+y\in U\)
STEP 3Scalar
\(\lambda x\in U\)
SetSubspace?
y-axis in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)✓
null space \(\{x: Ax=0\}\) · \(\operatorname{span}(x_1,\dots,x_m)\)✓
square \(-1\le x,y\le 1\)✗ scaling leaves it
Quick ways to fail: \(x+y=1\) (misses 0) · \(x\ge0\) (fails \(\lambda=-1\)) · \(xy=0\) (fails addition: \((1,0)+(0,1)\)).

2.5 · Span slide 18 · Lecture 2a notes

\(\operatorname{span}(S)\) = every vector you can build from S: \(\alpha x+\beta y+\dots\)
\(S\)\(\operatorname{span}(S)\)Shape
\(\{(1,0)\}\)\(\{(\alpha,0)\}\) = x-axisline
\(\{(0,0)\}\)\(\{(0,0)\}\)point
\(\{(1,2),(1,1)\}\)all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)plane
\(\{(1,1),(2,2)\}\)line through \((1,1)\)line
Watch out: adding a dependent vector does not make the span any bigger.

LECTURE 3 Independence via coefficients, basis, dimension

3.1 · Independence through coefficients slides 3–7

If each \(x_i\) is built from independent \(b\)'s, test the coefficient columns instead.
\(x_1=b_1-2b_2+b_3-b_4,\;\dots\) → columns of coefficients: \[\begin{bmatrix}1 & -4 & 2 & 17\\ -2 & -2 & 3 & -10\\ 1 & 0 & -1 & 11\\ -1 & 4 & -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 & -7\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -15\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -18\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\] Column 4 has no pivot → \(x_4=-7x_1-15x_2-18x_3\) → not independent

3.2 · Solutions of \(Ax=0\) form a subspace slides 8–11

Adding or scaling solutions of \(Ax=0\) gives another solution.
\[\begin{aligned}A(x+y)&=Ax+Ay=0+0=0\\ A(\alpha x)&=\alpha(Ax)=\alpha\,0=0\\ A\,0&=0\end{aligned}\]
So the null space is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The same argument works for \(U=\operatorname{span}(x_1,\dots,x_m)\).

3.3 · Basis & dimension slides 12–13

A basis builds the whole space with no extra vectors. Dimension = how many vectors are in it.
RULE 1Spans V
RULE 2Linearly independent
\(U=\{(a,b,0)\}\): basis \(\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}\),  \(\dim U=2\)
Add \((1,1,0)\) → still spans, but now dependent → not a basis.
A basis is not unique (ℝ³)
\(\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}\)  or  \(\{(1,0,0),(1,1,0),(1,1,1)\}\)
Watch out: dim counts basis vectors, not entries. Also, basis ≠ basic variable.

3.4 · Basis of \(\operatorname{span}(x_1,\dots,x_m)\) slides 14–15

  1. Put the vectors in as columns
  2. Reduce to REF
  3. Pivot columns → take those original vectors
\(\mathbb{R}^5\) example (slide 15): pivots in columns 1, 2, 4 \[\text{basis}=\{x_1,x_2,x_4\},\qquad \dim U=3,\qquad x_3=-x_1+2x_2\]
Rank–Nullity check
\(\operatorname{rank}(A)+\operatorname{nullity}(A)=n\) (number of columns)
Watch out: never use the reduced columns as the basis.

LECTURE 4 Linear maps, eigenvalues, decompositions

1 · Is T linear? slide 3

Linear = T respects adding and scaling. Always run the same three tests.
STEP 1Zero
\(T(0)=0\)?
STEP 2Addition
\(T(x+y)=T(x)+T(y)\)?
STEP 3Scalar
\(T(\alpha x)=\alpha T(x)\)?
Tiny example: \(T(a,b,c)=(a+2b,\;bc,\;3a-c)\)
Zero ✓  ·  Addition ✗ \(x=(0,1,0),y=(0,0,1)\)  ·  Scalar ✗ \(x=(0,1,1),\alpha=2\)
→ not linear. Red flags: + constant, \(ab\), \(c^2\), \(|a|\).
Watch out: passing the zero test proves nothing. Show numbers for every ✗.

2 · Trace slide 4

\(\operatorname{Tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{Tr}A+\operatorname{Tr}B\) · \(\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha A)=\alpha\operatorname{Tr}A\) · \(\operatorname{Tr}(I_n)=n\) · \(\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\operatorname{Tr}(BA)\)

3 · Characteristic polynomial slide 5

Subtract λ from the diagonal only, then take the determinant.
\[p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I),\qquad A-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}a-\lambda & b\\ c & d-\lambda\end{bmatrix}\]
2 × 2 shortcut
\(p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\operatorname{Tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det(A)\)
Tiny example
\(A=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 4\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow \lambda^2-4\lambda-5=(\lambda-5)(\lambda+1)\)

4 · Eigenvalues & eigenvectors slides 6–9

An eigenvector keeps its direction. A only stretches it by λ.
  1. \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) → the λ's
  2. Reduce \(A-\lambda I\) → basic / free, free = \(t\)
  3. \(x=t(\dots)\), clear fractions, \(x\neq0\)
  4. Verify \(Ax=\lambda x\) with the original A
  5. Check: Σλ = Tr A, Πλ = det A, rank + nullity = n
\(A=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 4\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\): \(\lambda=5\to(1,1)\),   \(\lambda=-1\to(-2,1)\)
Watch out: no zero row after reducing means λ is wrong. Moving a term across = flips its sign.

5 · Eigenspace & spectrum slides 10–11

The eigenspace is all eigenvectors for one λ (plus 0). Its dimension = number of free variables.
\[A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 2 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{spectrum}=\{2,3\}\] \(E_2\): free \(a=s,\;b=t\) → basis \(\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}\), dim 2
\(E_3\): free \(c=t\) → basis \(\{(1,0,1)\}\), dim 1
Transpose
\(A\) and \(A^{\top}\): same eigenvalues, eigenvectors can differ.
Watch out: give each free variable its own letter (s, t). Spectrum lists each value once.

6 · Distinct λ → independent slide 12

Different stretch amounts force different directions. n distinct λ give a basis of ℝⁿ.
Test
Put the eigenvectors as columns of P, reduce, and look for a pivot in every column.
\[P=\begin{bmatrix}\boxed{1} & 1 & 1\\ 0 & \boxed{1} & 2\\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{2}\end{bmatrix}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{3 pivots}\Rightarrow\text{independent}\]
Watch out: this only works one way. A repeated λ may still have independent eigenvectors.

8 · Diagonalization slides 14–16

Diagonal matrices are easy. Seen through its eigenvectors, A becomes diagonal.
\[D=\begin{bmatrix}d_1 & 0\\ 0 & d_2\end{bmatrix}:\;\det=d_1d_2,\;\;D^k=\begin{bmatrix}d_1^k & 0\\ 0 & d_2^k\end{bmatrix},\;\;D^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac1{d_1} & 0\\ 0 & \tfrac1{d_2}\end{bmatrix}\]
  1. P = eigenvectors as columns
  2. D = eigenvalues in the same order
  3. det P ≠ 0, then verify \(AP=PD\) → \(D=P^{-1}AP\)
\(A=\begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\): \(P=\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), \(D=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 5\end{bmatrix}\), det P = −3
Watch out: \(\begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}\) has too few eigenvectors, so it can't be diagonalized.

9 · Eigendecomposition & powers slides 17–20

Flip \(D=P^{-1}AP\) into \(A=PDP^{-1}\). For powers, the middle pieces cancel.
\[A^2=PD\underbrace{P^{-1}P}_{I}DP^{-1}=PD^2P^{-1}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\]
Tiny example
\(A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\), \(D=\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) → \(A^3=P\begin{bmatrix}27 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}14 & 13\\ 13 & 14\end{bmatrix}\)
Watch out: only D gets the power, not P.

10 · Symmetric: \(A=PDP^{\top}\) slide 21

Use unit eigenvectors. Then \(P^{\top}P=I\), so the inverse is just the transpose.
\[A=\begin{bmatrix}5 & -2\\ -2 & 2\end{bmatrix},\quad P=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2\\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix},\quad D=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 6\end{bmatrix}\]
Where \(\tfrac1{\sqrt5}\) comes from: \(\|(1,2)\|=\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt5\).
Watch out: without \(\tfrac1{\sqrt5}\) you get \(P^{\top}P=5I\), not I.

11 · Cholesky \(A=LL^{\top}\) slides 22–23

A "square root" of a symmetric positive-definite matrix. Fill L one entry at a time in this order:
① l₁₁00
② l₂₁④ l₂₂0
③ l₃₁⑤ l₃₂⑥ l₃₃
\(l_{11}=\sqrt{a_{11}}\) · \(l_{21}=\dfrac{a_{21}}{l_{11}}\) · \(l_{31}=\dfrac{a_{31}}{l_{11}}\)
\(l_{22}=\sqrt{a_{22}-l_{21}^2}\) · \(l_{32}=\dfrac{a_{32}-l_{31}l_{21}}{l_{22}}\) · \(l_{33}=\sqrt{a_{33}-l_{31}^2-l_{32}^2}\)
Tiny example
\(\begin{bmatrix}9 & 3 & 6\\ 3 & 5 & 10\\ 6 & 10 & 45\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow L=\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 & 0\\ 1 & 2 & 0\\ 2 & 4 & 5\end{bmatrix}\)
Watch out: in \(l_{32}\), subtract \(l_{31}l_{21}\) before dividing.

LECTURE 5 QR, SVD, approximation

12 · QR by Gram–Schmidt slides 3–4

Make the columns perpendicular: remove the part of \(a_2\) that points along \(e_1\).
  1. \(e_1=a_1/\|a_1\|\)
  2. \(u_2=a_2-(a_2\cdot e_1)\,e_1\), then \(e_2=u_2/\|u_2\|\)
  3. \(Q=[\,e_1\;e_2\,]\),   \(R=Q^{\top}A\) (upper-triangular)
\(A=\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1\\ 4 & 7\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow Q=\tfrac15\begin{bmatrix}3 & -4\\ 4 & 3\end{bmatrix},\;R=\begin{bmatrix}5 & 5\\ 0 & 5\end{bmatrix}\)
Watch out: subtract along the unit vector \(e_1\), not \(u_1\).

13 · SVD \(A=U\Sigma V^{\top}\) slides 5–14

Any matrix = turn, then stretch, then turn.
  1. Compute \(A^{\top}A\) (symmetric)
  2. Its eigenvalues, largest first → \(\sigma=\sqrt{\lambda}\)
  3. V = unit eigenvectors (divide by ‖x‖, not σ)
  4. Σ = σ's on the diagonal, same size as A
  5. \(u_i=\frac1{\sigma_i}Av_i\) → U
\(A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 2\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\): \(A^{\top}A=\begin{bmatrix}5 & 3\\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}\), \(\lambda=8,2\), \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}2\sqrt2 & 0\\ 0 & \sqrt2\end{bmatrix}\), \(V=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\), \(U=I\)
Watch out: σ = √λ. For a 2×3 matrix, Σ gets an extra zero column.

14 · Matrix approximation slide 15

A is a stack of layers. Keep the big ones, drop the small ones.
\[A=\sigma_1u_1v_1^{\top}+\sigma_2u_2v_2^{\top}+\dots\qquad \hat A(k)=\text{first }k\text{ layers}\]
\(\begin{bmatrix}2 & 2\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\)= \(\begin{bmatrix}2 & 2\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) keep, σ₁ = 2√2+ \(\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\) drop, σ₂ = √2
Column × row (outer product)
\(\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_1 & v_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_1v_1 & u_1v_2\\ u_2v_1 & u_2v_2\end{bmatrix}\)
Share kept
\(\dfrac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}=\dfrac{8}{10}=80\%\)
Watch out: a scalar in front multiplies every entry and cancels none.
